Georgia's **2026 labour-migration reform** introduced something the country did not previously have in a systematic form: a **mandatory right to work** for foreign nationals. Where many people once simply worked on the strength of a residence permit or a long visa-free stay, the law now treats the **permission to work** as a distinct authorisation that must be obtained in its own right. This guide is about that authorisation alone — what it is, who needs it, who is exempt, how to get it, and what happens if you skip it. The companion [work residence permit guide](https://relocation.ge/en/work-residence-permit-georgia/) covers the immigration status that sits on top of the right to work, and the [guide to Georgian visas](https://relocation.ge/en/guide-to-georgian-visas/) covers the D1 immigration visa.

The rules below come from two layers. The **Law of Georgia on Labour Migration**, as consolidated to its **15 April 2026** version, sets the framework: it incorporates **Law No 862 of 26 June 2025** (the main reform, most provisions effective 1 March 2026) and **Law No 1509 of 15 April 2026** (which broadened the exemptions). The **procedure** sits in **Government Ordinance No 70 of 20 February 2026**, which was substantially rewritten by **Ordinance No 321 of 9 July 2026**, in force on publication the following day. Ordinance No 321 is where the practical detail now lives — the four-month cap on short-term professional activity, the employer quota, the turnover thresholds and the video interview for the self-employed.

Neither ordinance has an **official English translation**, and the English consolidated text of the Law on the Legislative Herald (matsne.gov.ge) lags the Georgian by roughly a year. For the current rules the **Georgian text is authoritative**, and the description below is our own reading of it — confirm the wording that applies to you before relying on it.

## What the right to work actually is

The "right to work" — in the statute, the right to carry out **labour activity** — is the permission for a foreign national to take paid employment or to carry out entrepreneurial activity for economic benefit in Georgia. The core rule is that **only a foreigner who holds the right to work _together with_ a qualifying immigration status** (a work residence permit, an IT-employed residence permit, a D1 immigration visa, or another residence permit) may lawfully work in Georgia — **except in the exceptional cases the Law itself provides** for. That "except" clause, added by Law No 1509, matters: the exemptions are part of the rule, not an afterthought.

Two things follow. First, a residence permit on its own **no longer grants the right to work**; the permission to work is separate. Second, the right to work is **not** an immigration status — it does not by itself let you live in Georgia. It is the labour-market authorisation that then unlocks the work residence permit or D1 visa.

The right to work is granted by the **Ministry of Internally Displaced Persons from the Occupied Territories, Labour, Health and Social Affairs of Georgia**, through its **electronic labour-migration system**. The residence permits that follow are issued by a different body, the **Public Service Development Agency** under the Ministry of Justice.

## Who counts as a labour immigrant, and who as self-employed

The Law draws a line between two kinds of foreign worker, and the application route differs for each.

A **labour immigrant** is a foreigner without a permanent-residence permit who is **employed by a local employer**, including under a remote-employment relationship with a local employer. A **self-employed alien** is a foreigner without a permanent-residence permit who, as an independent contractor, carries out trade, services or other entrepreneurial activity for financial benefit. Law No 1509 **removed the word "partner"** (პარტნიორი) from this self-employed definition, narrowing who is swept in through mere participation in a company.

## The exemptions: who does not need the right to work

This is the part most relocating workers care about, and it is where the April 2026 amendments did their main work. The Law sets out a list of categories that fall **outside** the right-to-work requirement, and Law No 1509 substantially expanded it. In substance, you are exempt if you are:

- a person granted **refugee, humanitarian (additional) or temporary protection** status in Georgia;
- a **registered asylum seeker**;
- a member of an **accredited diplomatic mission, foreign consulate or international organisation** in Georgia;
- an **accredited journalist** working for foreign media;
- a person covered by an **international treaty** that sets a different rule;
- a holder of a valid **investment residence permit**;
- a holder of a valid **special residence permit** (the kind issued on the written initiative of a member of the Government);
- a person engaged in **short-term professional activity** as defined by government decree;
- a person carrying out activity **for the benefit of a public institution** or a state-share enterprise;
- a person who works **completely remotely for an employer and does not need to enter Georgia** to perform the work;
- a person supplying **labour or services to a non-resident** in connection with that non-resident's activity carried on **outside Georgia** — in practice, freelancers and contractors with foreign clients;
- a person performing **managerial or governance functions** in a Category I, II or III enterprise, or in a **public-interest entity** (as classified under the Law on Accounting, Reporting and Audit), or on its audit committee;
- a person carrying out **cultural-heritage conservation, restoration or rehabilitation** works;
- a person holding the **right of temporary stay** in Georgia under Article 60 of the Law on the Legal Status of Aliens and Stateless Persons.

**Ordinance No 321 wrote this list into the procedure itself**, so the ordinance the Agency actually applies now mirrors the exemptions in the Law rather than lagging behind them. One carve-out is worth reading closely: the **foreign-client** exemption does **not** apply where the foreigner is an **individual entrepreneur who needs a residence permit in order to stay in Georgia lawfully**. A freelancer serving clients abroad while living in Georgia on an Individual Entrepreneur registration should therefore not assume the exemption covers them.

The two carve-outs that matter most for the typical remote worker or freelancer are the **fully-remote-work** exemption and the **foreign-client services** exemption. The reform was widely read at first as sweeping remote workers in; Law No 1509 walks that back and makes clear that work with no real footprint in the Georgian labour market is outside the regime. The boundary for someone **physically present in Georgia while serving only foreign clients** is still being settled in administrative practice, so treat the exemption as a strong basis but confirm your own situation before relying on it. The **tax** treatment of this kind of work — which is a separate question from the right to work — is covered in the [digital nomads guide](https://relocation.ge/en/georgia-for-digital-nomads/).

## Short-term professional activity: the four-month rule

"Short-term professional activity" was listed as an exempt category from the start, but until July 2026 nobody could say what it meant — the Law simply deferred to a government decree. **Ordinance No 321 supplies the definition**, and it comes with conditions.

The activity may not exceed **four months in total within a calendar year**, counted from the day the labour immigrant begins it, and it may **never run longer than the period of your lawful stay** in the country. The exemption is therefore a genuine short-visit tool, not a back door to indefinite work.

It is also **not automatic**. Before the activity starts, the **local employer or the organiser of the activity** must register the labour immigrant in the labour-migration electronic system at **labourmigration.moh.gov.ge** and submit the information prescribed by the ordinance (identification of the employer, of the person responsible for the filing, and of the worker, together with the profession, the position and the start and end dates). That **registration is free of charge**.

If the registration is missing, or the Agency has not confirmed its status, or the person is in Georgia **without a lawful basis**, or the activity falls on the **quota list** in Annex No 3, then the work **does not qualify** as short-term professional activity. In that case it is simply unauthorised work, and both the worker and the employer or organiser face liability under the Law on Labour Migration.

## How to apply

The mechanics differ by category of worker, but both routes go through the Ministry's **electronic labour-migration system**.

For an **employee**, the application is made by the **local employer** after an employment contract has been concluded — the employee does not file alone. The right to work that results is tied to that **specific employer**; it is not a portable permission you can carry to a different job.

For a **self-employed** person, the application is made **directly** by the individual — and since Ordinance No 321, **in person rather than through a representative**. The applicant must then pass a **compulsory video interview** with the Agency, conducted in **Georgian or English** (you may attend with an **interpreter**), holding the **original foreign passport** used for the application. The electronic application is confirmed at the end of that interview, and only then does the Agency decide. Failing to appear, failing to confirm the application, or failing to produce the original passport is now an express ground for refusal.

### Employer quotas and the turnover test

Ordinance No 321 also puts two employer-side conditions into the right-to-work stage.

An employer that plans to hire **more than five foreign nationals in a calendar year**, or whose foreign staff would exceed **5% of total headcount** — unless that 5% comes to fewer than five people — must **agree an annual quota** with the Agency electronically, **at least ten working days before** applying for the right to work. Applications during that year then sit inside the agreed quota, which can be raised on a reasoned request; a quota left substantially unused may be **cut the following year**.

The employer must also evidence, through the Revenue Service, an **annual turnover of at least GEL 50,000 for each foreign national** seeking the right to work. For **educational and medical institutions** the figure is **GEL 35,000**. A young company is given room to breathe: the turnover certificate is **not required** where the employer has **no more than three foreign employees** and **no more than three months** have passed since it was founded or registered. Employers not registered for VAT prove the same turnover by other competent certificates covering the preceding twelve months. Where the enterprise category matters, the Agency verifies it against data published on **reportal.ge** by the accounting and audit supervision service; an enterprise it cannot identify as a public-interest entity or Category I–III is treated as **Category IV**.

### Timing, documents and the fee

The Agency may ask for additional information or corrections. The review period is a **maximum of 30 calendar days** from the date a **complete** file is submitted — an incomplete file does not start the clock — or a **maximum of 10 working days** where the application is handled under the **expedited** procedure. The decision is issued and served **electronically**, and is treated as delivered the moment it is sent to the email address you gave; it can be appealed under the General Administrative Code.

Your **foreign passport must have at least six months of validity** remaining at the moment you apply.

The **service fee is capped at GEL 500**, and that cap **includes expedited handling**: there is no lawful higher fast-track price above it. The fee is paid to the **State Employment Support Agency**. Note that if you apply for someone the Law on Labour Migration does not cover at all, the Agency may simply leave the application **unexamined — and the fee is not refunded**.

## After you have the right to work

The right to work is the first step; an immigration status must follow, and the Law sets deadlines.

If you are **abroad**, you must apply for a **D1 immigration visa within 30 calendar days** of the right to work being granted. If you are **already in Georgia**, you must apply for a **work residence permit** — or, for IT-sector employees, the **IT-employed residence permit** — **within 10 calendar days**.

There is an important relief here: you are **not obliged** to obtain a residence permit if you **already hold any residence permit**, or if you **work completely remotely and do not need to enter Georgia**. That mirrors the exemption logic on the work side.

To **continue** an employment relationship, the employer must apply to **extend** the right to work **at least 30 calendar days before** it expires. The employer must also record an **early termination of, or change to, the employment agreement** in the electronic system **within 5 calendar days**. If the right to work is refused or terminated, the decision can be **appealed to a court within one month**, but there is no right to work in Georgia while that review is pending.

Ordinance No 321 draws a useful line around **changes to your job**. Changing the **position you hold**, or — for a self-employed person — the **field of activity**, still means obtaining the corresponding right to work afresh. But where a change to the employment contract alters only the **level of the position, the salary or the working schedule**, there is **no** obligation to obtain the right to work again, and no obligation to report the contract change to the electronic system.

## When the right to work can be refused

The Ministry may **refuse** the right to work where, among other grounds: an **extension** application is filed late; the foreigner's **profession, qualification and intended position do not match the needs of the Georgian labour market** or fall on the **restricted list** of activities; or there is an **expulsion** proceeding or decision against the person. The restricted-list and labour-market-needs grounds are the discretionary heart of the regime, so the match between your qualifications and the declared role matters.

Ordinance No 321 tightened the procedural grounds. An **incomplete** file is refused if the defect is not cured **within 10 calendar days** of being identified — or if it is not cured **properly at the first attempt**, which removes the room for repeated partial fixes. Refusal also follows where the number of foreign hires **exceeds the agreed quota**, or the role sits in a **quota-capped profession whose quota is already full**; where the documents evidencing the **activity criteria** or the other prescribed documents are **not submitted**; and, for a self-employed applicant, where they **do not attend the video interview**, cannot confirm the electronic application, or do not present the **original passport**.

## When the right to work ends

The right to work **terminates** on events such as: expiry or early termination of the **employment contract**; a self-employed person **leaving Georgia for more than six months**; expiry of the **D1 visa** or the **residence permit**; **failure to apply in time** for the visa or residence permit; a **negative decision** on the visa or residence permit; or an **expulsion** decision. After a refusal or termination, a fresh application may be made **no earlier than one month** after the decision, and only if the person is **outside Georgia** or otherwise has a **legal basis to stay**.

## The cost of getting it wrong

Working without the right to work is an **administrative offence with real fines**, and liability falls on both the foreign national and the employer. A foreigner working without the required right to work is treated as engaging in **unlawful labour activity** and cannot rely on the full protection of employment rights under Georgian law. The fines escalate with repetition:

| Violation | First | Repeat | Subsequent |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Foreign worker without a permit | GEL 2,000 | GEL 4,000 | GEL 6,000 |
| Self-employed foreigner (Individual Entrepreneur) without a permit | GEL 2,000 | GEL 4,000 | GEL 6,000 |
| Employer, per illegally employed foreigner | GEL 2,000 | GEL 4,000 | GEL 6,000 |
| Violating permit conditions (e.g. changing activity without updating the permit) | GEL 2,000 | GEL 4,000 | GEL 6,000 |
| Employer failing to report a termination, amendment or extension of a contract | GEL 1,000 | GEL 2,000 | GEL 3,000 |

Enforcement is coordinated. The authority administering work permits may **share information on violations with other state bodies** — including the **Labour Inspection Service** and migration authorities — so a single breach can surface across agencies. This makes the right to work a compliance issue for employers as much as for individuals. The penalty framework is set out in the Law on Labour Migration together with **Government Ordinance No 70 of 20 February 2026** on issuing the right to work.

## If you already live and work in Georgia

Because this regime is new, the Law includes a **transitional rule** for people already in the system. A labour immigrant who was **registered with active status** in the Ministry's database **as of 1 March 2026** must obtain **both the right to work and the corresponding residence permit by 1 January 2027**. If you are arriving fresh, or changing status, the ordinary rule applies: the right to work must be in place before you start the work that requires it.

## How this fits the bigger picture

The right to work is one layer of a two-stage system. Once you hold it, the next step is the immigration status: a **work residence permit** — which carries its own **income** threshold, at least five times the subsistence minimum in monthly income — or a **D1 immigration visa**. The full set of residence-permit types is in the [guide to residence permits](https://relocation.ge/en/guide-to-residence-permits/).

The **turnover** test now appears at **both** stages, and this changed in July 2026. It used to be described as belonging to the residence-permit stage alone; Ordinance No 321 wrote it into the right-to-work rules as well, so the same **GEL 50,000 per foreign national** (GEL 35,000 for educational and medical institutions) is tested when the right to work is sought. On the residence-permit side, the companion **Ordinance No 317 of 9 July 2026** made a matching change: a company with **no more than three foreign employees** that was registered **no more than three months ago** is excused from the turnover certificate for a first work residence permit — but that permit is then issued for only **six months**. Details are in the [work residence permit guide](https://relocation.ge/en/work-residence-permit-georgia/).

## A note on a new and moving framework

The mandatory right-to-work system is recent and **still moving**. In little over a year it has been changed four times: **Law No 862 of 26 June 2025** (the reform itself), **Law No 1509 of 15 April 2026** (which softened the regime for fully remote workers and the self-employed), and then, on a single day, **Ordinances No 321 and No 317 of 9 July 2026**, which rewrote the procedure and took effect the next day. Figures such as the subsistence minimum, the quota annex and the restricted-activity list are updated separately and more often still.

The **English** consolidated text on matsne.gov.ge does not reflect the April 2026 amendments, and the July 2026 ordinances have **no official English version at all** — the account above is our reading of the Georgian originals, which are the authoritative text. Given how fresh these rules are, administrative practice on the edges (particularly the remote-work and foreign-client exemptions, and how the video interview is run) is still settling. Confirm the current requirements, fees, processing times, exemptions and transitional deadlines through the Ministry of Labour's [labour-migration system](https://www.labourmigration.moh.gov.ge/) and the Public Service Development Agency before acting.

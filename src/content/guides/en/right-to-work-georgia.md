Georgia's **2026 labour-migration reform** introduced something the country did not previously have in a systematic form: a **mandatory right to work** for foreign nationals. Where many people once simply worked on the strength of a residence permit or a long visa-free stay, the law now treats the **permission to work** as a distinct authorisation that must be obtained in its own right. This guide is about that authorisation alone — what it is, who needs it, who is exempt, how to get it, and what happens if you skip it. The companion [work residence permit guide](https://relocation.ge/en/work-residence-permit-georgia/) covers the immigration status that sits on top of the right to work, and the [guide to Georgian visas](https://relocation.ge/en/guide-to-georgian-visas/) covers the D1 immigration visa.

The rules below come from the **Law of Georgia on Labour Migration** as consolidated to its **15 April 2026** version, which incorporates the amendments made by **Law No 862 of 26 June 2025** (the main reform, most provisions effective 1 March 2026) and **Law No 1509 of 15 April 2026** (which broadened the exemptions). The **English** consolidated text on the Legislative Herald (matsne.gov.ge) lags the Georgian by roughly a year, so for the current rules the **Georgian consolidated text is authoritative**.

## What the right to work actually is

The "right to work" — in the statute, the right to carry out **labour activity** — is the permission for a foreign national to take paid employment or to carry out entrepreneurial activity for economic benefit in Georgia. The core rule is that **only a foreigner who holds the right to work _together with_ a qualifying immigration status** (a work residence permit, an IT-employed residence permit, a D1 immigration visa, or another residence permit) may lawfully work in Georgia — **except in the exceptional cases the Law itself provides** for. That "except" clause, added by Law No 1509, matters: the exemptions are part of the rule, not an afterthought.

Two things follow. First, a residence permit on its own **no longer grants the right to work**; the permission to work is separate. Second, the right to work is **not** an immigration status — it does not by itself let you live in Georgia. It is the labour-market authorisation that then unlocks the work residence permit or D1 visa.

The right to work is granted by the **Ministry of Internally Displaced Persons from the Occupied Territories, Labour, Health and Social Affairs of Georgia**, through its **electronic labour-migration system**. The residence permits that follow are issued by a different body, the **Public Service Development Agency** under the Ministry of Justice.

## Who counts as a labour immigrant, and who as self-employed

The Law draws a line between two kinds of foreign worker, and the application route differs for each.

A **labour immigrant** is a foreigner without a permanent-residence permit who is **employed by a local employer**, including under a remote-employment relationship with a local employer. A **self-employed alien** is a foreigner without a permanent-residence permit who, as an independent contractor, carries out trade, services or other entrepreneurial activity for financial benefit. Law No 1509 **removed the word "partner"** (პარტნიორი) from this self-employed definition, narrowing who is swept in through mere participation in a company.

## The exemptions: who does not need the right to work

This is the part most relocating workers care about, and it is where the April 2026 amendments did their main work. The Law sets out a list of categories that fall **outside** the right-to-work requirement. Law No 1509 expanded this list to **fourteen items** (lettered ა through ნ in the Georgian text). In substance, you are exempt if you are:

- a person granted **refugee, humanitarian (additional) or temporary protection** status in Georgia;
- a **registered asylum seeker**;
- a member of an **accredited diplomatic mission, foreign consulate or international organisation** in Georgia;
- an **accredited journalist** working for foreign media;
- a person covered by an **international treaty** that sets a different rule;
- a holder of a valid **investment residence permit**;
- a holder of a valid **special residence permit** (the kind issued on the written initiative of a member of the Government);
- a person engaged in **short-term professional activity** as defined by government decree;
- a person carrying out activity **for the benefit of a public institution** or a state-share enterprise;
- a person who works **completely remotely for an employer and does not need to enter Georgia** to perform the work;
- a person supplying **labour or services to a non-resident** in connection with that non-resident's activity carried on **outside Georgia** — in practice, freelancers and contractors with foreign clients;
- a person performing **managerial or governance functions** in a Category I, II or III enterprise (as classified under the Law on Accounting, Reporting and Audit) or on its audit committee;
- a person carrying out **cultural-heritage conservation, restoration or rehabilitation** works.

The two carve-outs that matter most for the typical remote worker or freelancer are the **fully-remote-work** exemption and the **foreign-client services** exemption. The reform was widely read at first as sweeping remote workers in; Law No 1509 walks that back and makes clear that work with no real footprint in the Georgian labour market is outside the regime. The boundary for someone **physically present in Georgia while serving only foreign clients** is still being settled in administrative practice, so treat the exemption as a strong basis but confirm your own situation before relying on it. The **tax** treatment of this kind of work — which is a separate question from the right to work — is covered in the [digital nomads guide](https://relocation.ge/en/georgia-for-digital-nomads/).

## How to apply

The mechanics differ by category of worker, but both routes go through the Ministry's **electronic labour-migration system**.

For an **employee**, the application is made by the **local employer** after an employment contract has been concluded — the employee does not file alone. The right to work that results is tied to that **specific employer**; it is not a portable permission you can carry to a different job.

For a **self-employed** person, the application is made **directly** by the individual, and the right to work is tied to the **specific specialty or field** of activity declared.

In both cases the Ministry may ask for additional information or for corrections to the application. The review period is a **maximum of 30 calendar days** counted from the date a **complete** set of information and documents has been submitted — so an incomplete file does not start the clock.

The **service fee is capped at GEL 500**, and that cap **includes expedited handling**: there is no lawful higher fast-track price above it. The fee is paid to the **State Employment Support Agency**.

## After you have the right to work

The right to work is the first step; an immigration status must follow, and the Law sets deadlines.

If you are **abroad**, you must apply for a **D1 immigration visa within 30 calendar days** of the right to work being granted. If you are **already in Georgia**, you must apply for a **work residence permit** — or, for IT-sector employees, the **IT-employed residence permit** — **within 10 calendar days**.

There is an important relief here: you are **not obliged** to obtain a residence permit if you **already hold any residence permit**, or if you **work completely remotely and do not need to enter Georgia**. That mirrors the exemption logic on the work side.

To **continue** an employment relationship, the employer must apply to **extend** the right to work **at least 30 calendar days before** it expires. The employer must also record an **early termination of, or change to, the employment agreement** in the electronic system **within 5 calendar days**. If the right to work is refused or terminated, the decision can be **appealed to a court within one month**, but there is no right to work in Georgia while that review is pending.

## When the right to work can be refused

The Ministry may **refuse** the right to work where, among other grounds: the application is **incomplete** and the gaps are not fixed within the 10 days allowed; an **extension** application is filed late; the foreigner's **profession, qualification and intended position do not match the needs of the Georgian labour market** or fall on the **restricted list** of activities; or there is an **expulsion** proceeding or decision against the person. The restricted-list and labour-market-needs grounds are the discretionary heart of the regime, so the match between your qualifications and the declared role matters.

## When the right to work ends

The right to work **terminates** on events such as: expiry or early termination of the **employment contract**; a self-employed person **leaving Georgia for more than six months**; expiry of the **D1 visa** or the **residence permit**; **failure to apply in time** for the visa or residence permit; a **negative decision** on the visa or residence permit; or an **expulsion** decision. After a refusal or termination, a fresh application may be made **no earlier than one month** after the decision, and only if the person is **outside Georgia** or otherwise has a **legal basis to stay**.

## The cost of getting it wrong

Working without the right to work is an **administrative offence with real fines**. Carrying out, or organising, labour activity without the required right to work is fined **GEL 2,000 for the employer or organiser** and **GEL 2,000 for the labour immigrant or self-employed person**; a **repeat** offence **doubles** the fine, and a further repeat **triples** it. **Changing employer or field of activity without renewing** the right to work is separately fined **GEL 2,000**. **Obstructing** a labour-migration inspection or **failing to present identity documents** carries its own fines — for employers these are scaled by the size of the business (broadly GEL 1,000 to GEL 7,000), and for a self-employed person around GEL 5,000. These figures make the right to work a compliance issue for employers as much as for individuals.

## If you already live and work in Georgia

Because this regime is new, the Law includes a **transitional rule** for people already in the system. A labour immigrant who was **registered with active status** in the Ministry's database **as of 1 March 2026** must obtain **both the right to work and the corresponding residence permit by 1 January 2027**. If you are arriving fresh, or changing status, the ordinary rule applies: the right to work must be in place before you start the work that requires it.

## How this fits the bigger picture

The right to work is one layer of a two-stage system. Once you hold it, the next step is the immigration status: a **work residence permit** (which carries its own income and turnover thresholds — at least five times the subsistence minimum in monthly income, and GEL 50,000 annual employer turnover per applicant, with education and medical institutions exempt from the turnover test) or a **D1 immigration visa**. Those thresholds belong to the residence-permit stage, not to the right to work itself, and are explained in the [work residence permit guide](https://relocation.ge/en/work-residence-permit-georgia/). The full set of residence-permit types is in the [guide to residence permits](https://relocation.ge/en/guide-to-residence-permits/).

## A note on a new and moving framework

The mandatory right-to-work system is recent and still being implemented in practice. It has already been amended — by **Law No 862 of 26 June 2025** and then by **Law No 1509 of 15 April 2026**, which softened the regime for fully remote workers and the self-employed — and figures such as the subsistence minimum and the restricted-activity list are periodically updated. The **English** consolidated text on matsne.gov.ge does not yet reflect the April 2026 amendments, so rely on the **Georgian** consolidated text for the current rules. Confirm the current requirements, fees, processing times, exemptions and transitional deadlines through the Ministry of Labour's labour-migration channels and the Public Service Development Agency before acting.

**Georgian** (*kartuli*) is the **state language** of Georgia and the everyday language of most of the population. This guide is an orientation for people settling in Georgia: what the language and alphabet are like, when residents actually need Georgian, and the **state, university and independent options** for learning it. It is **general information, not legal advice**, and describes programmes **neutrally** without endorsing any commercial course. Course availability, levels and fees change, so confirm details with the **Ministry of Education, Science and Youth** and the institution concerned.

## The language and the alphabet

Georgian is a **Kartvelian** language — a small family native to the Caucasus that is not related to Indo-European, Turkic or Semitic languages, which is part of why it feels unfamiliar to most newcomers. Its closest relatives are other Kartvelian languages such as Megrelian, Laz and Svan.

Modern Georgian is written in the **Mkhedruli** script, with a few features that make it approachable:

- **33 letters**, written **left to right**;
- **no capital letters**;
- a **close, consistent match between letters and sounds**, so words are largely pronounced as written; and
- **familiar punctuation** (full stops, commas, question marks).

Many learners find the **alphabet quicker to master than expected**, while the **grammar** — noun cases and a rich verb system — takes longer. The **living culture of the three Georgian writing systems** is inscribed on **UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity**, a reflection of how central the script is to Georgian identity.

## When residents actually need Georgian

**For everyday life** in Tbilisi, Batumi and other larger centres, many foreign residents get by with **English and Russian**, particularly in banking, hospitality, IT and international business. The picture changes **outside the cities**, and whenever you deal with **public offices, healthcare, courts, schools and neighbours**, where Georgian is far more useful. Even a working grasp of the alphabet and basic phrases noticeably smooths daily life.

**For naturalisation**, Georgian is a **formal requirement**. Under the **Organic Law of Georgia on Georgian Citizenship**, applicants for citizenship must pass a **state-run test that includes knowledge of the state language of Georgia**, within limits set by the authorities — normally alongside tests on the **history of Georgia** and the **basic principles of law**. The test is taken **before** the citizenship application is submitted. How this fits into the wider process is covered in **[Georgian Citizenship & Naturalisation](/en/georgian-citizenship-and-naturalisation)**; confirm the current test format and any exemptions with the **Public Service Development Agency**.

## State and civic-integration programmes

Several **state programmes** teach Georgian, though most are aimed primarily at **Georgian citizens from minority-language communities** (for example Armenian- and Azerbaijani-speaking communities) as part of **civic integration**, rather than at newly arrived foreign nationals. They are useful to understand as the public framework.

- **Zurab Zhvania School of Public Administration (ZSPA).** This LEPL under the Ministry of Education runs a **State Language Teaching and Integration Program** with **levelled courses (A1 upward)** and related civic-education modules, delivered at the school and its regional branches. The school also publishes **free learning materials**, including audio lessons and posters.
- **The "1+4" Georgian Language Preparatory Programme.** Administered within the state higher-education system, this gives **citizens who graduated from non-Georgian-language schools** a **preparatory year of intensive Georgian** before continuing to a four-year undergraduate programme. It is designed to widen access to higher education for ethnic-minority citizens; it is **not** a general course for foreign relocators.

Eligibility, intake periods and locations vary between programmes and change over time — check directly with the **Ministry of Education, Science and Youth** and the institution running the course.

## University language centres and independent options

For **foreign residents** (including those without Georgian citizenship), the usual routes are:

- **University language centres.** Several **state and private universities** teach **Georgian as a foreign language**, sometimes at multiple levels and with certificates. Offerings and intake differ by university.
- **Independent tutors and language schools.** Widely available, especially in Tbilisi and Batumi, and flexible on schedule and level.
- **Self-study.** The **alphabet plus graded materials** — including the **free resources** published by the Zurab Zhvania School — can take you a long way for reading, pronunciation and everyday phrases.

This guide does **not** endorse any particular commercial provider. Because levels, formats and fees change frequently, confirm current options and prices **directly** with the university or school you are considering.

## Practical tips

- **Start with the alphabet.** Because spelling closely tracks pronunciation, learning the 33 letters unlocks reading signs, menus and names quickly.
- **Prioritise high-frequency phrases** — greetings, numbers, directions, shopping and pharmacy vocabulary — for immediate daily payoff.
- **Match the course to your goal.** Casual daily life needs different material from a **naturalisation language test**; if citizenship is your aim, confirm the test scope with the **Public Service Development Agency** and choose materials accordingly.
- **Practise locally.** Georgian speakers generally respond warmly to foreigners making an effort, which makes everyday practice one of the most effective tools.

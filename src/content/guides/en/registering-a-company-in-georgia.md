Setting up a company in Georgia is governed by the Law of Georgia on Entrepreneurs (No 875 of 2021, in force since 1 January 2022). The law is open to foreigners: foreign individuals and foreign-registered legal entities can found and own a Georgian company on the same terms as locals, and there is no residency requirement to be a partner or a director.

## Business forms

Business activity can be carried out either as an [individual entrepreneur](/en/register-individual-entrepreneur-remotely) or as a company. The law recognises five company forms: the general partnership, the limited partnership, the limited liability company (LLC), the joint-stock company (JSC) and the cooperative. A company's brand name must indicate its form — for example "LLC" or "Limited Liability Company". For most foreigners setting up a small or medium business, the LLC is the standard choice.

## Comparison of company forms

| Form | Founders | Liability of partners | Capital / ownership | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| General partnership (GP) | Two or more partners | Unlimited — each partner is personally liable for company debts | Contributions; all partners manage the business | Partnerships based on high mutual trust |
| Limited partnership (LP) | At least one general and one limited partner | General partners unlimited; limited partners limited to their investment | Contributions; only general partners manage | Bringing in investors who do not manage |
| Limited liability company (LLC) | One or more persons (individuals or legal entities) | Limited to each partner's contribution | Capital divided into shares; no minimum amount | Most small and medium businesses |
| Joint-stock company (JSC) | One or more shareholders | Limited to the shares held | Capital divided into shares that can be traded, including publicly | Larger, investment-heavy or scalable businesses |
| Cooperative | Members | Limited for members | Membership contributions | Mutual economic benefit, e.g. agriculture or joint production |

### General and limited partnerships

A general partnership is formed by two or more partners who run a business together; all of them take part in management and each is personally liable for the company's obligations. A limited partnership combines two kinds of partner: general partners, who manage the company and carry unlimited liability, and limited partners, who contribute capital, take no part in management, and are liable only up to their investment.

### Joint-stock company

A joint-stock company is usually used for larger businesses. Its capital is divided into shares that can be transferred and, in some cases, traded publicly. Shareholders are not liable for the company's debts beyond their shares. A JSC has a more elaborate governance structure than an LLC, typically involving a director or directors and a supervisory board. A dedicated guide for the joint-stock company is not published here; this section summarises its main features.

### Cooperative

A cooperative is a company created for the mutual economic benefit of its members, who cooperate towards shared goals. Members have limited liability. Cooperatives are commonly used in agriculture and joint production, where the emphasis is on collaboration rather than profit maximisation.

## Liability: the key difference between forms

The forms differ mainly in how partners answer for the company's debts. Partners of an LLC, a JSC and a cooperative, and the limited partners of a limited partnership, are not liable to creditors for the company's obligations — their risk is limited to their investment. By contrast, the partners of a general partnership, and the general partners of a limited partnership, are jointly, severally and personally liable with all their assets. This is why the LLC, with its limited liability, is so widely used.

## The limited liability company in detail

The LLC is a separate legal entity: it owns its own assets, signs contracts in its own name, and is responsible for its own obligations, independently of its owners. It can be founded by one or more persons — individuals or legal entities — there is no maximum number of partners, and there is no residency requirement.

Ownership is divided into shares. A partner's shareholding determines their voting power and, unless the charter provides otherwise, their share of profits. The charter can set different rules for profit distribution.

As a general rule, partners are not personally liable for the LLC's obligations. The main exception is where a partner abuses the company — for example through fraud or misuse — in which case a court may "pierce the corporate veil" and hold that partner personally liable.

## Capital

For an LLC, the law does not prescribe a minimum capital amount. The capital is divided into shares, and the partners' shareholdings are expressed in percentages that together equal 100%. A company may also set an authorised capital — a ceiling for issuing further shares in future — but it is not obligatory.

## Management structure

An LLC has a simple and flexible management system with two levels:

- **General meeting of partners** — the company's highest governing body. It approves major decisions, amends the charter, appoints and removes directors, and approves the financial results.
- **Director(s)** — responsible for day-to-day management. The director represents the company externally, signs contracts, and acts on its behalf. There can be one or several directors.

## The charter

An LLC must have a charter (company statute) that regulates how the company operates. It usually sets out the company name, the registered address, the partners and their shares, the management structure, and the decision-making rules.

## How registration works

Registration of a company is mandatory and is carried out by the registration authority, the National Agency of Public Registry. Crucially, registration entails both state registration and tax registration in a single step: the registration authority sends the data electronically to the Revenue Service, so there is no separate tax-registration procedure. A company is deemed established from the moment of registration, and the registration decision takes effect once it is published on the authority's central electronic platform. After registration, a company may apply for a preferential tax status — see [business statuses in Georgia](/en/business-statuses-georgia) for the small business, micro business and other regimes.

### State fee

| Registration speed | State fee |
| --- | --- |
| Within one business day | GEL 200 |
| On the day of filing | GEL 400 |

## What you need to register

To register a company you submit its instrument of incorporation (the charter or partners' agreement), which must set out the brand name, the legal address, the identification details of each partner or founder, and the person with management and representation powers (and a supervisory board, if there is one). The legal address must be the company's actual address in Georgia. The typical document set for an LLC includes:

- identification documents of the founder or founders;
- the company charter;
- information about the director;
- a legal address in Georgia;
- a translator where foreign participants are involved, so that documents can be signed in a language they understand.

Once registered, the company's data is public — anyone can view the registry and obtain an extract, and the documents filed at registration are published online free of charge.

## Foreign companies and redomiciliation

A company registered abroad can operate in Georgia through a branch, which is registered with the same authority. The law also allows an entrepreneur registered in another country to redomicile its registration to Georgia, moving the company itself rather than setting up a new one.

Once the company exists, see [running a company in Georgia](/en/running-a-company-in-georgia) for the governance and ongoing obligations that follow.

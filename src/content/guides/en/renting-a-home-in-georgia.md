Most people relocating to Georgia rent a home before they **[buy property](/en/buying-property-in-georgia-for-foreigners)**. Residential tenancies are governed by the **Civil Code of Georgia**. The law gives landlords and tenants considerable freedom to agree the terms of a lease, while also providing important default rules on matters such as rent, deposits, notice and termination.

## Rent and increases

The landlord and tenant are free to agree the initial rent and the mechanism for any future increases, including indexation or cost-of-living adjustments.

The Civil Code also recognises a tenant's refusal of a rent increase that reflects the current market level as one of the circumstances that may constitute a legitimate ground for termination by the landlord. In practice, however, any proposed increase remains a matter for negotiation between the parties.

## Deposits

Georgian law does not require a security deposit. If the parties agree to one, it may not exceed **three months' rent**.

The Civil Code provides that statutory interest accrues on the deposit throughout the tenancy. When the lease ends, the deposit should be returned to the tenant together with any accrued interest, unless it is lawfully set off against unpaid rent, utility charges or other amounts due under the lease. Any agreement reducing these statutory protections to the tenant's detriment is void.

In practice, landlords commonly request a deposit of one or two months' rent, and tenancy agreements do not always address the statutory interest requirement.

## Lease term

A residential lease may be agreed for either a **fixed term** or an **indefinite period**.

If the agreement does not specify a duration, it is treated as an indefinite tenancy. Either party may terminate an indefinite tenancy at any time by giving **three months' written notice**, unless the parties have agreed otherwise where permitted by law.

A fixed-term tenancy ends automatically when the agreed term expires, unless the contract provides otherwise. If the tenant continues to occupy the property after expiry and the landlord does not object, the lease is generally regarded as continuing for an indefinite period.

A tenant may also request that a fixed-term lease continue as an indefinite tenancy by giving written notice at least **two months** before the expiry of the fixed term, provided the landlord agrees.

Special rules apply to leases exceeding **ten years**, where the statutory right of termination becomes available only after the initial ten-year period has elapsed.

## Ending the lease early — tenant

A tenant may generally terminate a fixed-term lease before its expiry by giving at least **one month's written notice** and proposing a **financially reliable replacement tenant** who is acceptable to the landlord and willing to assume the remaining lease obligations.

The tenant may also terminate immediately where the landlord fails to hand over the property (in whole or in part), where the tenant is subsequently deprived of the agreed right to use the property, or where the condition of the property creates a significant risk to health or safety.

## Ending the lease early — landlord

Before the end of a fixed term, a landlord may terminate the lease only where a legitimate legal ground exists.

Examples include:
- a substantial breach of the tenancy agreement by the tenant;
- the landlord requiring the property for their own residential use or that of a close family member;
- the tenant refusing a market-based rent increase in circumstances recognised by the Civil Code; or
- unlawful or seriously improper conduct by the tenant that makes continuation of the lease unreasonable.

The landlord may also terminate where:

- rent remains unpaid for **three consecutive months**; or
- after receiving a warning, the tenant substantially damages the property or creates a clear risk of substantial damage.

For furnished residential accommodation, the Civil Code provides additional termination rights, including termination on **three months' written notice**.

## Residence permits and your legal address

If you intend to apply for a **Georgian residence permit**, remember that you will normally need to register a **residential (legal) address** in Georgia before your residence card can be issued.

If you are renting, discuss this with the landlord **before signing the lease**. Make sure the landlord is willing to allow you to register the rented property as your residential address and, if necessary, provide any declarations or supporting documents required for the registration process.

It is good practice to record this expressly in the tenancy agreement to avoid misunderstandings later.

## Form, condition and disputes

Notice terminating a residential lease must be given **in writing**.

At the end of the tenancy, the tenant must return the property in the condition in which it was received, allowing for ordinary wear and tear, or in the condition otherwise agreed in the lease.

Residential tenancy disputes are resolved through the ordinary Georgian courts unless the parties have agreed to arbitration.

## Practical tips

Before signing a tenancy agreement:

- confirm that the person renting the property is entitled to do so;
- make sure the rent, deposit, utility payments and payment dates are clearly stated;
- record the condition of the property with photographs or a written inventory;
- clarify who is responsible for utility bills and how they will be transferred or paid;
- if you may later apply for a residence permit, ensure the landlord agrees to let you register the property as your legal address; and
- keep a signed copy of the tenancy agreement and any payment records.

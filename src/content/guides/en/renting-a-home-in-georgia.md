Most people relocating to Georgia rent before they [buy](/en/buying-property-in-georgia-for-foreigners), and residential tenancies are governed by the Civil Code of Georgia (Articles 531–575). The rules give the parties wide freedom to agree terms, but they also set important defaults — especially around notice and termination — that are worth knowing before you sign.

## Rent and increases

Landlord and tenant are free to negotiate the initial rent and any later increases, including cost-of-living adjustments. One point favours the landlord: if the tenant refuses a proposed increase that reflects current market rates, the landlord is treated as having a legitimate reason to terminate the tenancy. In a market where supply is ample, however, an unreasonable increase is often self-defeating for the landlord.

## Deposits

The law does not require a deposit. If the parties agree to one, it may not exceed three times the monthly rent. Statutory interest must accrue on a deposit, and on termination the deposit is returned to the tenant together with that interest (or set off against outstanding rent or utilities). Any agreement that departs from this to the tenant's detriment is void. In practice, landlords commonly ask for around two months' rent, and the interest requirement is frequently ignored even though it is mandatory.

## Lease term

Georgian law does not require a tenancy to run for any particular period. If the contract does not state a term, it is deemed indefinite. An indefinite tenancy can be ended at any time by either party on three months' notice, with no other condition — which is why most tenancies in practice are fixed-term.

A fixed-term tenancy ends automatically when the term expires, and no notice is needed at expiry. If the tenant keeps using the property after the term ends and the landlord does not object, the contract is treated as extended for an indefinite term. A tenant on a fixed term can ask to extend it to an indefinite term by giving written notice at least two months before the end, if the landlord agrees. For very long fixed terms, a three-month notice is mandatory only where the term exceeds ten years, and then only after the ten years have passed.

## Ending the lease early — tenant

A tenant can normally terminate before the end of a fixed term by giving at least one month's notice and offering a solvent substitute tenant who is acceptable to the landlord and willing to take over the remainder of the term. The tenant may also terminate without observing the notice period if the property is not handed over (in whole or in part), if the tenant is later deprived of the right to use it, or if the home's condition creates a significant hazard to health.

## Ending the lease early — landlord

A landlord may terminate before expiry only for a legitimate reason. These include a substantial breach by the tenant; the landlord needing the home for themselves or close relatives; the tenant refusing a market-rate rent increase; or an illegal or immoral act by the tenant that makes continuing impossible. Separately, the landlord may terminate if rent goes unpaid for three consecutive months, or if the tenant — having ignored a warning — substantially damages the property or creates a clear danger of substantial damage. Where the home is let furnished, the landlord may always terminate on the three-month notice period.

## Form, condition and disputes

Termination of a tenancy must be made in writing. At the end of the tenancy the tenant must return the property in the condition it was received, allowing for normal wear and tear, or in the condition agreed in the contract. There are no special tenancy tribunals in Georgia: disputes are handled by the ordinary courts unless the parties have agreed to arbitration.

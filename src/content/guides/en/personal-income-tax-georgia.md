Georgia's personal income tax is unusually simple to state and unusually easy to misunderstand. The rate is a **flat 20%**, and the system is **territorial** — Georgia generally taxes individuals only on **Georgian-source income**. The misunderstanding lies in what counts as "foreign" income: for someone living and working in Georgia, far less of it is foreign-source than they expect. This guide sets out the rate, the territorial principle and the all-important **sourcing rules**, checked against the **primary Georgian law** — chiefly Articles 79–82 and 104 of the Tax Code.

## The rate: a flat 20%

Georgia taxes personal income at a **single flat rate of 20%**. There are **no progressive brackets** for individuals: employment income, most self-employment income and other taxable personal income are all taxed at 20%.

Employment income is typically taxed by **withholding at source** — the employer withholds and remits the tax — so many employees never file a return. Individuals with income that is **not** taxed at source generally file an **annual personal income tax return**.

Lower rates exist, but they belong to **separate regimes** (the small-business rules below), not to the standard schedule.

## Territorial taxation: only Georgian-source income

The defining feature of the Georgian system for individuals is that it is **territorial**. The mechanics sit in two articles of the Tax Code:

**Article 82** exempts the income of a **resident natural person** that is **not from a Georgian source** — so a resident individual's genuinely foreign-source income is, as a rule, outside the Georgian personal income tax base.

**Article 79** makes a **non-resident** taxable **only on Georgian-source income**.

Put together, the practical result is that **both residents and non-residents are taxed by Georgia on Georgian-source income**, and **foreign-source income of a resident individual is normally exempt**. This is what people mean when they call Georgia a territorial-tax jurisdiction.

Whether you are a resident or a non-resident in the first place is decided separately — see the [tax residency guide](https://relocation.ge/en/tax-residency-georgia/).

## The trap: what counts as "Georgian source"

The exemption for foreign income is real, but it turns entirely on the **source** of the income — and source is defined by **Article 104** of the Tax Code, not by where your client sits or where you are paid.

The guiding principle is **where the activity is performed and where the payer or asset is located**. Income from **work or services performed in Georgia**, income from **property located in Georgia**, and certain payments made by Georgian residents are **Georgian-source**. The crucial consequence for the remote-work crowd: if you are **physically in Georgia doing the work**, that income is typically **Georgian-source and taxable**, even if your customer, your invoice and your bank account are all abroad.

Income that is more likely to be genuinely **foreign-source** tends to be **passive** — for example, dividends from a foreign company or rent from property abroad. Even then, the categories in Article 104 are specific, so the sourcing of any particular income stream should be **checked against the text**, ideally with an adviser, rather than assumed.

## The low-rate alternative: small-business status

Because work performed in Georgia is usually Georgian-source and would otherwise meet the 20% rate, many freelancers and remote workers use Georgia's **small-business regime** instead. A resident individual can register as an **[Individual Entrepreneur](https://relocation.ge/en/register-individual-entrepreneur-remotely/)** and, if eligible, obtain **Small Business Status**, which taxes qualifying **turnover at 1%** (rising to **3%** above the turnover ceiling) in place of the standard 20% personal income tax on profit.

This is an **opt-in regime** with its own eligibility conditions and a list of excluded activities, and it is administered separately. It does not change the 20% standard rate — it offers a different basis of taxation for those who qualify. The dedicated guide covers registration and the conditions in detail.

## Filing, deadlines and getting it right

The **tax year is the calendar year**. Employment income is generally handled through **withholding**; individuals with income not taxed at source typically file an **annual personal income tax return by 1 April** of the following year. Your exact filing duty depends on the type of income you receive, so confirm it with the **Revenue Service** or an adviser.

Two cross-cutting points are worth keeping in view. First, your **[residency status](https://relocation.ge/en/tax-residency-georgia/)** and the **source** of each income stream together determine what Georgia taxes — neither question can be answered alone. Second, where foreign income or foreign tax is involved, Georgia's **[double-tax treaties](https://relocation.ge/en/double-tax-treaties-georgia/)** may change the outcome.

This guide is general information, **not personal tax advice**. Income sourcing and the small-business rules are fact-specific and the figures can change, so take professional advice before relying on a particular treatment.

[Registering a company](/en/registering-a-company-in-georgia) is only the start. The Law of Georgia on Entrepreneurs (No 875 of 2021) also sets out how a company is governed day to day and what obligations continue once it is operating. The rules give partners flexibility through the charter, while fixing certain protections that cannot be bargained away.

## Governing bodies

A company is run through up to three bodies: the general meeting of partners, the management body (the director or directors), and a supervisory board. The supervisory board is not universal — it exists only where the law or the company's charter requires it. In a typical LLC, the partners decide key matters at the general meeting and delegate day-to-day running to the management body.

## The general meeting

All partners have the right to take part in the general meeting. A company must hold a regular general meeting at least once a year, no later than six months after the annual balance sheet is drawn up, and the management body is responsible for convening it. Decisions are generally taken by a majority of votes, unless the law or the charter requires a higher threshold. Where a company has a single partner, that partner exercises the powers of the general meeting and records decisions in writing.

## The management body and directors' duties

The management body represents the company and runs its operations. Managers owe their duties to the company, and where an obligation is breached through the action or omission of more than one manager, they are jointly and severally liable to the company. Decisions of the general meeting taken within its authority are binding on the partners and the company's bodies.

## Keeping the registry current

Registered data must be kept accurate. Changes to the information held in the registry are filed with the registration authority, and because the registry is public, those changes are visible to anyone who looks. Managers must also notify the authority of significant events — for example the closure of a branch, the start or end of liquidation, the identity of liquidators, or the commencement of insolvency proceedings. Business letters of a limited partnership, an LLC or a JSC must carry the company's basic identifying data.

## Financial disclosure

Companies prepare annual financial statements that underpin the regular general meeting. Larger companies and certain branches face additional disclosure: a branch of an LLC or a JSC, for instance, must publish the company's financial statements drawn up, audited and disclosed under the relevant legislation. Detailed accounting and audit thresholds sit in separate legislation, but the general meeting cycle is built around the annual accounts.

## Reorganisation and redomiciliation

The law provides structured routes to restructure a company: conversion (changing legal form), merger and division. In a merger, the surviving company assumes all obligations of the merging companies; in a division, the recipient or newly formed companies are jointly and severally liable for the divided company's obligations, limited to the net assets each receives. Creditors are protected — within three months of a reorganisation being registered, a creditor can require security for claims that predate the reorganisation if they show it puts satisfaction of those claims at risk. The same chapter allows an entrepreneur registered abroad to redomicile its registration to Georgia.
